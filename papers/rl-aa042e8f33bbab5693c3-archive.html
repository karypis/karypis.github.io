Understanding Evolving Graph Structures for Large Discrete-Time Dynamic Graph Representation

www.vldb.org · 📄 Open PDF
TL;DR - What it does: This paper introduces UnderGS, a scalable GPU-resident framework for discrete-time dynamic graph (DTDG) representation that replaces adjacency-matrix neighbor management with a temporal-cohesive neighbor store, achieving up to 9× training speed-up and 31.36% average accuracy improvement over nine baselines across eight datasets. - Why it's important: Adjacency-matrix-based neighbor management for DTDGs consumes O(T|V|²) memory, prevents GPU-native batch processing, and captures only binary connectivity — making existing methods unable to scale to graphs with millions of edges or to faithfully represent structural evolution across snapshots. - How it does it: A GPU key-value store retains the top-K neighbors per node scored by a composite temporal influence score combining structural cohesiveness (node importance via subgraph density), path-length decay (α₁^(l-1)), and snapshot recency decay (α₂^(t-t')), together with a late-snapshot gradient aggregation mechanism that prevents information leakage during batch training. - The good: UnderGS is the only evaluated method that scales to the Stack-Overflow (2.6M nodes, 63M edges, 92 snapshots) and Wiki-Talk (1.1M nodes, 7.8M edges) datasets without out-of-memory errors, while simultaneously outperforming all baselines on both AUC and MRR tasks. - The missing: All experiments run on a single RTX 3090 (24 GB); the paper makes no claim about multi-GPU performance, and scalability beyond this hardware — or behavior on graphs with denser temporal dynamics than the tested datasets — is unevaluated. - Read it or not: Worth reading for researchers and engineers working on DTDG representation learning, temporal GNNs, or scalable graph ML infrastructure; it provides a principled, hardware-aware alternative to adjacency-matrix neighbor management with strong empirical evidence.

TL;DR - What it does: This paper introduces UnderGS, a scalable GPU-resident framework for discrete-time dynamic graph (DTDG) representation that replaces adjacency-matrix neighbor management with a temporal-cohesive neighbor store, achieving up to 9× training speed-up and 31.36% average accuracy improvement over nine baselines across eight datasets. - Why it's important: Adjacency-matrix-based neighbor management for DTDGs consumes O(T|V|²) memory, prevents GPU-native batch processing, and captures only binary connectivity — making existing methods unable to scale to graphs with millions of edges or to faithfully represent structural evolution across snapshots. - How it does it: A GPU key-value store retains the top-K neighbors per node scored by a composite temporal influence score combining structural cohesiveness (node importance via subgraph density), path-length decay (α₁^(l-1)), and snapshot recency decay (α₂^(t-t')), together with a late-snapshot gradient aggregation mechanism that prevents information leakage during batch training. - The good: UnderGS is the only evaluated method that scales to the Stack-Overflow (2.6M nodes, 63M edges, 92 snapshots) and Wiki-Talk (1.1M nodes, 7.8M edges) datasets without out-of-memory errors, while simultaneously outperforming all baselines on both AUC and MRR tasks. - The missing: All experiments run on a single RTX 3090 (24 GB); the paper makes no claim about multi-GPU performance, and scalability beyond this hardware — or behavior on graphs with denser temporal dynamics than the tested datasets — is unevaluated. - Read it or not: Worth reading for researchers and engineers working on DTDG representation learning, temporal GNNs, or scalable graph ML infrastructure; it provides a principled, hardware-aware alternative to adjacency-matrix neighbor management with strong empirical evidence. Problem & Motivation: Discrete-time dynamic graphs model systems (retail, finance, social networks) that evolve through discrete snapshots, where each snapshot is a static graph. The central challenge for representation learning is neighbor management: existing approaches either maintain a full adjacency matrix per snapshot — consuming O(T|V|²) memory and restricting scalability — or preprocess multiple adjacency matrices into node sequences for sequence models, incurring hours of preprocessing and CPU–GPU transfer overhead (e.g., FALCON requires 2.2 hours on Reddit-title). Both lines fail simultaneously: snapshot-focused GNN approaches lose cross-snapshot temporal context, while sequence-based approaches lose intra-snapshot structural context. Neither line scales to graphs with millions of nodes or hundreds of snapshots because they cannot batch-process efficiently on a single GPU. The gap is a neighbor management strategy that captures structural evolution, preserves temporal context, and fits in GPU memory without preprocessing. Key Idea: The core insight is to replace the adjacency matrix with a GPU-resident key-value store that, for each node, retains exactly K neighbors selected by a temporal influence score — a single scalar that fuses three quantities: structural importance of the neighbor (cohesiveness of its local subgraph), path-length discount (closer hops score higher), and snapshot recency discount (more recent interactions score higher). Because the store caps at K entries per node, total memory drops from O(T|V|²) to O(|V|K), independent of snapshot count. The store is maintained incrementally as new snapshots arrive: L-hop traversals compute influence scores for candidates; new neighbors displace incumbents if their scores are higher; a snapshot decay pass reduces all stored scores at snapshot boundaries. This design allows direct GPU access for message passing without preprocessing, and the influence score serves as an aggregation weight replacing degree normalization. Methodology: UnderGS consists of four interacting components. (1) Temporal influence score: For node vᵢ(t) and neighbor vⱼ(t') interacting over l hops, Iᵢⱼ(t) = Cⱼ(t') · α₁^(l-1) · α₂^(t-t'), where Cⱼ(t') is the node importance defined as a sum of exponential terms over the interaction counts within vⱼ's subgraph, α₁ ∈ (0,1) is the path-decay hyperparameter, and α₂ ∈ (0,1) is the snapshot-decay factor. Node importance distinguishes structurally cohesive neighbors from peripheral ones, which degree alone cannot. The paper proves three formal properties: Snapshot Decay (Theorem 4.6 — more recent neighbors are preferred when structurally comparable), Cohesive Priority (Theorem 4.7 — neighbors in cohesive subgraphs are retained over sparser ones), and Path Decay (Theorem 4.8 — direct neighbors score higher than equidistant distant ones). (2) Temporal-cohesive neighbor store: A GPU key-value store with O(1) lookup and update per node; initialized from scratch and updated incrementally per batch or snapshot; reduces storage from O(T|V|²) to O(|V|K). K=16 and L=2 in all experiments. (3) Time-aware filter: For nodes with sparse current-snapshot interactions, historical neighbors can dominate. A Bernoulli mask with probability ρ (tuned by dataset density: ρ=0.3 for high density, ρ=0.5 for low density) randomly zeros out historical neighbor influence scores before aggregation, shifting focus toward current interactions. (4) Late-snapshot gradient aggregation: Each snapshot G(t) is partitioned into B(t) mini-batches; losses are computed independently per batch using binary cross-entropy on link prediction labels, but gradients are accumulated across all batches of the snapshot and applied once after the full snapshot is processed (θ ← θ − η · (1/B(t)) ΣᵦᵢB(t) ∇L^b_{G(t)}). This prevents later batches from being influenced by parameter updates from earlier batches within the same snapshot (information leakage). The framework is compatible with six neural network architectures (GCN, GIN, GAT, GraphSAGE, MLP Mixer, Transformer), using influence scores as aggregation weights in lieu of degree normalization; message embedding Mᵢⱼ(t) = [Zⱼ(t-1) ‖ eᵢⱼ(t') ‖ φ(t-t')]Wₘ concatenates node embedding, edge features, and a time encoding. Key Results: Evaluated on 8 datasets spanning multiple domains and scales (UCI-Message: 1,899 nodes; Stack-Overflow: 2.6M nodes, 63M edges, 92 snapshots). Baselines are 9 models: 4 GNN-based (EvolveGCN-H, EvolveGCN-O, Roland, WinGNN) and 5 sequence-based (DGNN-LSTM, DGNN-GRU, DGNN-Transformer, SimpleDyG, FALCON). All experiments use a single GeForce RTX 3090 (24 GB RAM). Key quantitative outcomes: (1) AUC (link prediction): UnderGS achieves best results on 7/8 datasets; up to 13.56% improvement over the best baseline; e.g., 99.97% AUC on Bitcoin-OTC vs. 99.75% for second-best. Only Roland is competitive on small datasets. (2) MRR (link ranking): UnderGS achieves best on all 8 datasets; average 59.20% improvement; e.g., 95.01% MRR on Bitcoin-Alpha vs. 36.20% for second-best (SimpleDyG). (3) Training efficiency: Up to 9× overall speed-up vs. best baseline; up to 6× per-epoch speed-up. (4) Scalability: 4 of 9 baselines (DGNN variants, FALCON) OOM on 5+ datasets; only UnderGS completes all 8 including Stack-Overflow and Wiki-Talk. (5) vs. TGL (multi-GPU framework): UnderGS achieves 94× per-epoch sampling speed-up, 10× overall training speed-up, and 24.07% better AUC+MRR on average — while running on a single GPU. Ablation (Table 3): removing snapshot decay drops MRR by up to 82.30%; removing path decay drops by up to 26.02%; replacing node importance with k-core drops MRR by 9.29% on average; the time-aware filter contributes 4.35% on average. Training pipeline ablation (Table 4): pipeline yields 6.93–10.40× overall speed-up and 3.65–10.44× per-epoch speed-up vs. snapshot-by-snapshot training. Novelty Classification: NEW METHOD + NEW SYSTEM/TOOL + ENGINEERING OPTIMIZATION. The temporal influence score combining structural cohesiveness, path decay, and snapshot decay as a unified neighbor selection criterion is genuinely new; the GPU-resident incremental neighbor store that replaces adjacency matrices in GNN training is a novel infrastructure contribution; the late-snapshot gradient aggregation addressing information leakage in DTDG mini-batch training is a targeted engineering advance. The individual components (influence-weighted aggregation, GPU key-value stores, gradient accumulation) are known; the novelty is their integration into a principled, theoretically grounded neighbor management framework for DTDGs. Rigor & Reproducibility: Ablations are thorough: 5 structural variants tested across all 8 datasets (Table 3); training pipeline ablation on 2 datasets (Table 4); compatibility tested across 6 neural networks with and without influence scores (Figures 6–7). Experiments are repeated 10 times and mean ± standard deviation is reported. Code and data are publicly released at https://github.com/dani0108/UnderGS (PVLDB Artifact Availability badge confirmed). The paper was published in PVLDB Vol. 19, No. 5, 2026 (peer-reviewed). Hyperparameters (K=16, L=2, α₁=0.1, α₂=0.05, filter ratio 40%) are reported; ρ is density-dependent (ρ=0.3 or 0.5) which introduces a tuning step not fully automated. The embedding dimension is fixed at 128 and batch size at 200 for all models, ensuring fair comparison. Theoretical proofs for the three core properties are deferred to a technical report (Sections 2.1 and 2.2 thereof); the main paper contains proof sketches, which limits independent verification from the paper alone. Deployability: (4-property check):** Profile: EMPIRICAL + SYSTEMS (built framework evaluated by deployment on real datasets). - Practical: Runs on a single commodity GPU (RTX 3090, 24 GB) on all 8 tested datasets including the 63M-edge Stack-Overflow graph. - Efficient: Neighbor store reduces memory from O(T|V|²) to O(|V|K); eliminates preprocessing (2.2 hours for FALCON on Reddit-title); achieves 6–9× training speed-up over the best baselines; per-epoch sampling is 94× faster than TGL. - Effective: Outperforms all 9 baselines on AUC (7/8 datasets) and MRR (8/8 datasets) with statistically meaningful margins (error bars reported); ablations confirm each component contributes materially, not just the aggregate. - Scalable: Tested at up to 2.6M nodes and 63M edges (Stack-Overflow), 7.8M edges with 324 snapshots (Wiki-Talk); no experiment exceeds this scale; behavior at 10×–100× scale on billion-edge graphs is not tested, though the O(|V|K) theoretical bound is favorable. The paper delivers a software artifact (GitHub repository with source code and data). Strengths: - The temporal influence score is principled and theoretically grounded with three proven properties (Theorems 4.6–4.8), giving the neighbor selection mechanism a formal foundation absent in prior adjacency-matrix approaches. - The scalability gap versus competitors is substantial and not marginal: UnderGS is the only method completing all 8 datasets on a single GPU, including graphs where every alternative OOMs; the 94× sampling speed-up over TGL (a multi-GPU system) is the most striking empirical result. - Compatibility with six heterogeneous neural architectures (message-passing and non-message-passing) without architectural modifications demonstrates genuine generality of the neighbor store abstraction. - The late-snapshot gradient aggregation addresses a concrete information-leakage failure mode in DTDG batch training that prior work ignores, and ablation (Table 4) shows it yields 6.93–10.40× speed-up over snapshot-by-snapshot training. Author-Acknowledged Limitations: The paper does not include a dedicated limitations section. The conclusion notes that UnderGS targets single-GPU settings, contrasting it with multi-GPU approaches; no explicit failure modes or scope boundaries are stated. The density-dependent filter probability ρ is acknowledged implicitly as a dataset-specific hyperparameter but not discussed as a limitation. The authors do not discuss behavior on highly skewed degree distributions (power-law graphs), adversarially structured temporal graphs, or directed weighted graphs beyond those tested. Analyst-Identified Gaps: - Hyperparameter sensitivity for ρ: The time-aware filter probability is set per dataset based on an undisclosed density threshold (10⁻⁴). The paper does not report a sensitivity analysis for ρ, leaving unclear how sensitive performance is to this choice and how a practitioner would set it without labeled validation data. - Single-hardware evaluation: All experiments use one specific GPU. The O(|V|K) memory claim is theoretically favorable, but the paper does not demonstrate behavior when the neighbor store itself exceeds GPU memory (e.g., billion-node graphs), nor does it discuss CPU-offload strategies for this regime. - Transductive setting only: All downstream tasks (link prediction, link ranking) are evaluated on edges within the training graph's node set. Inductive generalization to entirely unseen nodes at inference time is not evaluated, limiting applicability to dynamic graphs with frequent node arrivals. - Theoretical proof completeness: The three key theorems are proved in a separate technical report not included in the PVLDB submission; the main paper contains only proof sketches, which a peer reviewer cannot independently verify from the paper alone. - No comparison to hyperbolic or geometry-aware DTDG methods (e.g., the authors' own prior work on hyperbolic temporal graphs, refs [23, 47]) which have shown strong performance on sparse social graphs — an omission that slightly narrows the baseline comparison. Bottom Line: A practitioner building DTDG representation learning pipelines gains a concretely useful contribution here — UnderGS offers a principled, GPU-efficient replacement for adjacency-matrix neighbor management with strong empirical support across 8 diverse datasets and publicly released code, making it the current method of choice for single-GPU DTDG training at scale.

Where does output diversity collapse in post-training?

arxiv.org · 📄 Open PDF
TL;DR - What it does: This paper demonstrates, through a controlled comparison of three parallel post-training lineages of the Olmo 3 7B model, that output diversity collapse is determined by training data composition—not post-training algorithm—and cannot be recovered at inference time. - Why it's important: Inference-time scaling methods (pass@k, majority voting, self-consistency) depend on output diversity; if post-training eliminates that diversity, the scaling benefits are lost, and value-laden tasks risk homogenized outputs. - How it does it: The paper exploits Olmo 3's public checkpoints across three lineages—Think (narrow two-teacher CoT distillation), Instruct (broad multi-source), and RL-Zero (RL directly from base)—that share the same SFT→DPO→RL recipe but differ in data, then measures diversity at each checkpoint across 15 tasks with four metrics, and surgically removes CoT at inference to separate format from weights. - The good: The CoT suppression experiment (Think-not-thinking) is definitive: removing chain-of-thought format costs 18–32% accuracy on hard tasks but leaves SBERT diversity unchanged at every stage, proving the collapse is weight-embedded, not format-imposed. - The missing: The study is purely observational within existing checkpoints; no direct data composition intervention is run, so the causal claim that "more teachers → less collapse" is supported by cross-lineage comparison rather than a controlled experiment. - Read it or not: Read it if you work on LLM post-training, inference-time compute scaling, or alignment diversity; it provides concrete numbers and a clean experimental argument that inference-side fixes for diversity collapse are insufficient.

Problem & Motivation: Post-trained LLMs produce less varied outputs than their base counterparts, and this homogenization undermines inference-time scaling methods—pass@k, majority voting, and self-consistency—that require diverse samples to aggregate over. The problem extends to creative and value-laden tasks where output homogenization risks imposing a single perspective. Prior work attributed collapse to specific algorithms (DPO in narrative generation, SFT in reasoning, reward in creative tasks) without disentangling whether the algorithm or the training data caused the drop, and without testing whether the chain-of-thought generation format itself constrains diversity at inference. Key Idea: When two models share identical post-training algorithms (SFT→DPO→RL) but differ only in data—narrow two-teacher CoT distillation versus broad multi-source instruction tuning—diversity collapses at different stages and by different magnitudes, proving that data composition, not algorithm, determines when and how sharply collapse occurs. The secondary insight is that suppressing CoT at inference on a collapsed model does not restore diversity, demonstrating the collapse is baked into learned weights rather than imposed by generation format. Methodology: The study evaluates 13 Olmo 3 7B checkpoints across three post-training lineages. Think uses SFT on ∼2.3M synthetic CoT traces from two teachers (QwQ-32B and DeepSeek-R1), followed by DPO on ∼200K Delta Learning pairs and GRPO-based RL on ∼105K prompts. Instruct initializes from Think-SFT, then trains on ∼2.2M multi-source examples (GPT-3.5, GPT-4, GPT-4.1) that strip reasoning traces, uses the same prompt pool for DPO but with GPT-judged pairs, and the same RL stage. RL-Zero applies RL directly to Base, bypassing SFT and DPO, in four domain-specific variants. Four complementary metrics are measured per checkpoint on 15 tasks across six categories: EAD (lexical, unique n-grams normalized for length bias), SBERT (semantic, mean pairwise cosine distance with all-mpnet-base-v2), NLI (logical, pairwise entailment/contradiction scoring with roberta-large-mnli), and Vendi Score (effective number of distinct semantic modes). K=16 outputs per prompt at T=0.6 are generated; for Think-lineage models, reasoning traces are stripped before computing metrics. A Think-not-thinking condition prefills an empty `<think>\n</think>` block to force direct answers, isolating format from weights. Quality-filtered diversity decomposes aggregate Da (SBERT on all outputs) into Dc (SBERT on correct-only subset) and Vc (Vendi Score on correct outputs) for six verifiable tasks. Three critical design decisions: (1) using matched temperature T=0.6 for all models including Base, validated in Appendix H to introduce only ∼11% SBERT reduction versus the 62% collapse observed at SFT—an order-of-magnitude smaller effect; (2) stripping CoT traces before any metric computation so diversity reflects final answers only; (3) using C13 13-gram matching to flag six benchmarks with 7–30% training overlap, though results on contaminated and clean tasks are consistent. Key Results: Think-SFT loses 62% of Base SBERT diversity on average across 15 tasks, versus 38% for Instruct-SFT. Despite initializing from the already-collapsed Think-SFT, Instruct-SFT recovers a median 40% of lost diversity due to its multi-source data. DPO removes 23% further diversity in Instruct versus only 4% in Think (which has little remaining to remove). RL-Zero retains ≥71% of Base diversity with a median of 94%. Both supervised lineages converge to similar final floors: Think retains 38% and Instruct 34% of Base diversity. The Vendi Score drops from ∼3.4 effective semantic modes (Base) to ∼1.8 at final checkpoints, with near-total collapse on math (GSM8K: 1.3 modes, MATH-Algebra: 1.4). EAD (lexical diversity) remains stable or increases even as SBERT drops, a dissociation replicated across all tasks—aligned models use varied vocabulary to express semantically identical content. Value-pluralism tasks show the steepest Think collapse (PRISM −78%, TruthfulQA −79%). The CoT suppression experiment finds zero diversity recovery on any task-stage combination: Think-not-thinking SBERT tracks Think within noise at every stage, while accuracy drops substantially (IFEval −8%, GSM8K −18%, HumanEval −32%, MATH-Geometry −32%). Quality decomposition reveals a task-dependent split: on IFEval, 83.4% of the diversity drop persists in Dc (genuine narrowing among correct outputs); on HumanEval, less than 10% is genuine (most collapse reflects elimination of wrong outputs). Think gains only +0.4 percentage points from majority voting on GSM8K (16 near-identical correct answers), while Base gains +24% and RL-Zero +22–26%. On TruthfulQA, majority voting hurts all models (majority vote@16 < accuracy@1) because collapse amplifies the dominant misconception. Novelty Classification: NEW EMPIRICAL FINDING. The contribution is a controlled dissociation of post-training algorithm effects from data composition effects on output diversity, plus the CoT-suppression falsification test. The experimental apparatus (multi-lineage comparison with identical recipes but different data, plus out-of-distribution inference interventions) is methodologically sharper than prior work, which confounded method and data. The quality-filtered decomposition framework is a useful analytical tool but draws on established ideas. The finding that both supervised lineages converge to the same diversity floor regardless of training trajectory is counter-intuitive and practically significant. Rigor & Reproducibility: Ablations are not applicable in the traditional sense—this is an observational study across existing checkpoints rather than a system with tunable components. The four-metric setup (EAD, SBERT, NLI, Vendi Score) provides complementary views, and the finding that EAD diverges from SBERT is itself a meaningful result. Training-evaluation contamination is measured and flagged; results are consistent across clean and contaminated benchmarks. Temperature sensitivity is assessed in a dedicated appendix and shown to be small relative to the observed effects. Code is publicly released at https://github.com/ckarouzos/where-diversity-collapses/. The LLM-as-judge evaluation (gpt-4.1-mini via OpenAI Batch API) for non-verifiable tasks introduces vendor dependence and known limitations for creative/value-laden tasks, which the authors acknowledge. No error bars or significance tests are reported on the main SBERT diversity comparisons; the study relies on consistent directional patterns across 15 tasks as evidence of reliability. This is a preprint under review. Deployability (4-property check): EMPIRICAL. - Practical: All experiments use vLLM and lighteval on standard HPC hardware; no proprietary infrastructure required beyond API access to gpt-4.1-mini for judge evaluation. - Efficient: The evaluation requires 13 models × 15 tasks × 500 prompts × 16 samples = ∼1.56M forward passes, plus embedding and NLI inference; substantial but within reach of academic compute with HPC access (acknowledged in the paper). - Effective: The evidence convincingly demonstrates that collapse is weight-embedded and data-driven for the specific Olmo 3 7B family; the practical implication—avoid narrow-teacher SFT—is well-supported within this scope. - Scalable: Tested exclusively at 7B; whether the same stage-attribution pattern holds at 70B+ is not examined and remains an open question. The convergence to a similar diversity floor across lineages might differ at scale. The work delivers a code artifact and data analysis rather than a deployable system or trained model. Strengths: - The parallel-lineage design is the strongest feature: Think and Instruct share identical algorithms and differ only in data, providing a cleaner causal structure for attribution than any prior single-lineage analysis. - The CoT-suppression experiment directly falsifies the hypothesis that generation format drives diversity collapse, resolving a confound present in all prior Think-vs-Instruct comparisons. - The quality-filtered decomposition (Da vs. Dc vs. Vc) is a concrete methodological contribution that resolves a genuine debate in the field about whether diversity collapse is "just quality control" or genuine homogenization—and shows it is task-dependent. - Breath of evaluation across 15 tasks spanning six qualitatively different categories produces a coherent picture of task-dependent diversity-quality tradeoffs. Author-Acknowledged Limitations: The authors note they measure final-answer diversity rather than reasoning-path diversity, which may underestimate collapse in the Think lineage where CoT traces are stripped. They distinguish distributional diversity (measured here) from representational diversity (which perspectives are preserved), noting that a model could appear collapsed distributionally while retaining key stances, or vice versa. LLM-judge evaluation of creative and value-laden tasks has known limitations, and those results are presented as supplementary context rather than primary evidence. Analyst-Identified Gaps: The study is observational within existing checkpoints; no direct data composition intervention (e.g., training on 4 vs. 2 vs. 10 teachers with fixed data volume) is run, so the causal mechanism connecting teacher breadth to collapse magnitude is inferred rather than established. The two teachers used for Think (QwQ-32B, DeepSeek-R1) come from the same training lineage and likely occupy a narrow region of output space; the paper does not characterize the diversity of teacher outputs themselves, which would strengthen the mechanistic interpretation. The study covers only 7B-scale models from a single family (Olmo 3); the convergence-to-same-floor finding could be specific to this architecture, pretraining mix, or scale. The RL-Zero diversity advantage (median 94% of Base retained) comes at a massive accuracy penalty (49.8–61% on GSM8K vs. 93% Think), and this tradeoff is described but not deeply analyzed—it is unclear whether entropy-regularized RL could preserve some RL-Zero diversity without the quality collapse. The paper does not examine whether simple interventions like increasing the number of SFT teachers from 2 to 8 would shift the diversity floor or just the trajectory. Bottom Line: A practitioner working on inference-time compute scaling, preference optimization, or model alignment will extract concrete and actionable findings here: diversity collapse is a data problem, not an algorithm problem, and you cannot fix it at inference time—read it.

Rethinking On-Policy Distillation of Large Language Models: Phenomenology, Mechanism, and Recipe

Problem & Motivation On-policy distillation uses the student's own sampled rollouts as training contexts and leverages the teacher's per-token log-probabilities as dense reward, avoiding the exposure-bias problem of supervised fine-tuning on fixed teacher sequences. Despite rapid adoption in industry post-training pipelines, OPD exhibits a striking failure mode: a stronger teacher can completely fail to improve the student while a weaker teacher succeeds. No prior work has systematically identified the conditions under which OPD succeeds or fails, nor explained the token-level mechanism through which teacher supervision steers the student. Practitioners therefore lack principled tools to select teachers, configure runs, or diagnose failures — and the implicit assumption that higher teacher benchmark scores guarantee better distillation is empirically wrong. Key Idea The central insight is that OPD's effective gradient signal is concentrated almost entirely on the small set of tokens that appear in both the student's and the teacher's high-probability top-k sets (the "overlap" region), which carries 97–99% of the combined probability mass. For OPD to succeed, (1) this overlap must be non-negligible from the outset (thinking-pattern consistency), and (2) the teacher must assign different probabilities to those shared tokens than the student already learned (new knowledge). When both conditions hold, the overlap set grows self-reinforcingly — each reverse-KL update pushes more student mass toward teacher-preferred tokens within the overlap, enlarging it further — creating a virtuous cycle. When either condition fails, the overlap stagnates and gradient updates point in incoherent directions despite the teacher's reward being globally informative. Methodology The paper is organized into three empirical components. Problem & Motivation: Training MoE language models with large expert counts from scratch is computationally expensive in a specific way: all expert weights, gradients, and optimizer states must reside in GPU memory even though only K of E experts are active per token, so memory and inter-device all-to-all communication both scale with total parameter count E, not active parameters K. Scaling laws show that lowering the activation ratio K/E improves quality-per-FLOP, which pushes practitioners toward very large E — but the cost of training from scratch grows proportionally. There is no existing method that grows expert count within an already-sparse MoE while preserving inference cost (top-K fixed), closing the gap to a from-scratch larger model within a limited training budget. Key Idea: Duplicate experts from a trained E-expert MoE checkpoint to construct a warm-initialized mE-expert model. Because the duplicated experts inherit learned weight vectors and the router is extended with near-identical entries (plus small bias noise), the expanded model starts at approximately the same loss as the source — far below random initialization. Loss-free load balancing then ensures every replica receives gradient signal, and stochastic gradient diversity drives replicas to specialize over subsequent CPT. The core insight is that warm initialization makes Term II of the quality-gap bound negative (closer to optimum than random init), which more than compensates for the time spent in a less expressive model class (Term I). Methodology: The pipeline has three stages. Phase 1: train an E-expert MoE for τ steps using a Warmup–Stable–Decay schedule with loss-free load balancing. Phase 2: apply the upcycling operator U_m — assign replication counts {r_e} to experts (uniform: r_e=m for all e; utility-based: higher counts for experts with larger gradient-norm scores ‖g_e‖²), copy each expert r_e times, extend the router by copying source logits and adding small uniform bias noise ε~U(−δ,δ) to replicated slots only. Phase 3: continue pre-training the mE-expert model for T−τ steps. Problem & Motivation: Autoregressive generation from large language models is memory-bandwidth-bound: each decoding step loads the full model weights to produce a single vector-matrix multiply, leaving GPU compute almost entirely idle. Speculative decoding (SD) amortizes target-model calls by having a cheap draft model propose K tokens that the target verifies in one forward pass via rejection sampling. The problem is that rejection truncates the draft at the first bad token, making throughput stochastic and highly sensitive to draft-target alignment. When sampling temperature rises or draft and target distributions diverge, the expected number of accepted tokens per round shrinks, and SD degrades toward the autoregressive baseline. The idle compute budget—arithmetic intensity for SD is 2B(K+1)/b FLOPs/byte, still far below the ridge points of 312, 412, and 584 for A100, H200, and B300 GPUs in FP16—remains wasted even with tree-based extensions. Key Idea: The core trick is recognizing that, because LLM inference is memory-bandwidth-bound, the additional arithmetic needed to score N particles simultaneously is nearly free—the GPU has the compute to spare. Replacing the binary accept/reject decision with continuous importance-weighted resampling across a particle population converts the stochastic throughput of SD (variable tokens produced per round) into a deterministic, fixed-size output (exactly K+1 tokens per particle per round), while reversing the guarantee: speed-up is now deterministic and approximation quality is stochastic (but bounded). KV-cache resampling is reduced from an O(ℓ_seq × d_model) tensor copy to an O(ℓ_seq) pointer-metadata update by exploiting prefix sharing. Methodology: The algorithm (Algorithm 1) maintains N weighted particles {(x^(n), w^(n))}. Each round: (1) Draft — each particle independently generates K tokens autoregressively from draft model q; (2) Score and bonus — target model p scores all N×K draft tokens and generates one bonus token per particle in a single batched forward pass; (3) Reweight — block weights update as the product of K token-level importance ratios p/q; (4) Resample — if the effective sample size ESS = (Σw^(n))²/Σ(w^(n))² falls below threshold η, particles are resampled proportional to weights via pointer exchanges on paged KV-cache metadata, resetting weights to 1/N. At generation end, one sequence is sampled from the terminal normalized weights. The arithmetic intensity of a full cycle is AI_cycle = 2BN(ρK+K+1)/(b(ρK+1)) ≈ 2BN(K+1)/b when ρ≪1, an ~N× improvement over standard SD. The three critical design decisions—(1) batching all N particles through the target in a single forward pass, (2) pointer-based rather than tensor-based KV resampling, and (3) ESS-triggered conditional resampling—are all well-justified by hardware arithmetic intensity analysis. The engine is built as a fork of SGLang, using dynamic batching, PagedAttention, and RadixAttention. Problem & Motivation Membership inference attacks (MIAs) determine whether a specific text appeared in a model's training data, with applications in copyright enforcement, privacy auditing under GDPR, and data governance compliance. All existing MIA methods for language models are hand-crafted heuristics — loss thresholding, Min-K%, Zlib normalization, reference-based calibration (RefLoss), and EZ-MIA — each designed by human intuition with performance bounded by the designer's insight. Learned MIAs outperform heuristics in other domains (image classifiers) but require training many shadow models with known membership, which is prohibitively expensive at LLM scale. The implicit assumption blocking learned MIA from scaling to language models has been that shadow model training is a prerequisite. Key Idea The single enabling insight is that fine-tuning any model on any corpus produces unlimited labeled membership data by construction — the identity of members and non-members is known exactly — eliminating the shadow model bottleneck entirely. The cost of generating training data is only forward passes through existing fine-tuned models, not training new ones. A second, independent insight is that training diversity (many model-dataset combinations at fixed total data volume) filters architecture-specific artifacts — tokenizer idiosyncrasies, logit scale biases — leaving only the invariant memorization signal: the relational shift in per-token likelihood a fine-tuned model assigns relative to its pre-trained baseline. This invariance holds because gradient descent on cross-entropy loss is the only shared mechanism across transformers, state-space models, linear attention, and gated recurrence. Methodology The LT-MIA pipeline has three stages. First, per-token feature extraction: given black-box access to the fine-tuned target model and its pre-trained reference (same architecture), 154-dimensional feature vectors are extracted at each of T=128 token positions. Features span three groups — target model features (45 channels: per-token cross-entropy loss, top/bottom-20 logits, ground-truth logit and log-normalized rank, global loss mean/std broadcast to all positions), reference model features (45 channels, same structure), and comparison features (64 channels: per-token loss difference, log-likelihood ratio, cross-model ranks for top-20 and bottom-20 tokens from each model). Logit features are locally normalized by subtracting the within-group maximum; rank features are log-transformed. Second, a lightweight transformer encoder (2 layers, 4 heads, feedforward dim 256, <500K total parameters) processes the T×154 feature sequence with sinusoidal positional encodings; a learned query computes a weighted sum over encoder outputs fed to a 2-layer MLP classifier predicting P(member). Third, training: 10 transformer-base models fine-tuned on 3 text corpora yield 30 model-dataset combinations (540,000 samples); binary cross-entropy is optimized with AdamW (lr=3×10⁻⁴, batch 1024, 30 epochs). Critical design decisions: (1) sequence modeling over positional features rather than aggregation to a scalar — contributes 5.0 AUC points over mean-pooled MLP, well-justified by classifier architecture ablation; (2) 30 diverse combinations over 1 combination at identical data volume — reduces train-to-eval AUC gap from 20.2 to 0.2 points, well-justified by diversity ablation; (3) comparison features dominate over target-only or reference-only features — permutation importance confirms Comparison > Target > Reference hierarchy identically across all four architectural families, substantiating the relational interpretation. Problem & Motivation: Diffusion language models offer a theoretically attractive alternative to autoregressive (AR) models — parallel token generation across a block avoids the sequential bottleneck of next-token decoding. Despite years of effort, DLMs consistently lag AR models of the same scale in quality, and their promised inference speedup rarely materializes in practice. Three distinct bottlenecks compound: (1) DLMs produce tokens under a generation distribution q that diverges from what the model would endorse under its own introspective (verify) distribution p, leading to incoherent multi-step reasoning; (2) block diffusion requires T denoising passes plus a KV-commit forward pass, capping tokens-per-forward (TPF) at N/2 even at perfect acceptance and incurring ~2–7× more FLOPs per decoded token than AR; (3) DLM inference is structurally incompatible with the paged-KV, continuous-batching, fused-attention machinery in modern AR serving stacks (SGLang, vLLM), preventing deployment-side optimization. Key Idea: AR models are intrinsically self-consistent: causal masking forces the model to predict token i+1 from hidden state at position i (logit shift), so the generation distribution q equals the verification distribution p by construction. Standard masked DLMs break this by training hidden[MASK_i] → token[i] (not i+1), destroying the inherent logit→token alignment. I-DLM's key insight is that restoring logit shift across both masked and clean positions, combined with all-masked training and strict causal attention, forces p ≈ q throughout training. This single structural alignment is sufficient to recover AR-level capability; no distillation, no curriculum, no separate verifier model is needed. Methodology: I-DLM has three interlocking components. (1) Introspective-consistency training: Given a sequence x₀, the model receives [x_t | x₀] as input, where x_t is fully masked. Strict causal attention is applied uniformly — position j attends only to positions i ≤ j — across both masked and clean regions. A logit shift is applied: hidden state at position i predicts token i+1, preserving the AR model's inherent logits[i] → token[i+1] mapping. Masked positions train a generation pathway (L_mask); clean positions train an introspection pathway (L_clean) recovering the exact causal anchor distribution p. An auto-balanced loss L = L_mask + ŝ · L_clean dynamically rescales via ŝ = L_mask/L_clean so both pathways receive equal effective gradient magnitude without manual tuning. (2) Introspective Strided Decoding (ISD): At each step, the algorithm appends N [MASK] tokens and runs a single forward pass that simultaneously generates N new draft tokens (from masked positions) and verifies N prior tokens against their causal anchor distributions (from clean positions) using the speculative-decoding p/q acceptance criterion. Accepted tokens advance; rejections trigger resampling and discard subsequent drafts. On full acceptance, a bonus token is emitted via the anchor distribution, yielding stride N+1. Because strict causal attention is used, the introspection pass piggybacks on the next stride step at zero additional cost — a 2N−1 query pass handles both. (3) AR-compatible serving: Because ISD uses strict causal attention with small extension sizes (≤9 tokens), it maps directly to SGLang's native extend mode, inheriting paged KV cache, continuous batching, and tensor parallelism. A stationary-batch scheduler reuses the batch object across ISD steps, bypassing full scheduler rebuild overhead. For lossless output (R-ISD), a gated LoRA residual activates only at [MASK] positions (base+LoRA for proposals, base-only for introspection), guaranteeing bit-for-bit identity with the base AR distribution. Critical design decisions: (i) all-masked training (instead of random-fraction masking) ensures 100% supervision density — well-justified by efficiency argument; (ii) auto-balanced loss — justified by empirical observation that masked losses dominate early training and starve the introspection pathway; (iii) strict causal (not block-causal) attention — justified by eliminating the KV-commit pass required by block diffusion and enabling AR serving stack reuse. The paper does not evaluate inference-time scaling behavior (e.g., does I-DLM benefit from repeated sampling or chain-of-thought expansion proportionally to AR?). Bottom Line: A practitioner building or deploying DLMs gains substantially from this paper: I-DLM is the first DLM to match same-scale AR quality, delivers real serving throughput advantages with standard infrastructure, and the introspective consistency analysis is the clearest account yet of why prior DLMs fall short.
